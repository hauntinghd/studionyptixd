# Skill 03 Companion — Beat Anatomy

This is the companion to Skill 03 (Script Writing). Skill 03 names the 12 beats of a long-form authority script. This file shows what each beat actually looks like in production-quality form.

The agent uses this when writing the deep middle of a script (beats 4-8), which is where most generated scripts collapse. Openers are easy. Closes are easy. The middle — where viewers quietly leave — is where the script wins or loses.

Each beat below contains:
1. **Function** — what the beat does for retention
2. **Inside structure** — how to build the beat
3. **Worked example** — a production-quality sample in V2 Federal Credentialed Expert (personal-finance authority channel IRS register, since it's our most-tested niche)
4. **Common failure modes** — what makes the beat collapse
5. **Self-check questions** — what to ask before moving on

V2 was chosen for consistency. The beat shapes apply across voices; the agent re-skins the worked example by swapping voice DNA. A V1 Documentary Authority version of beat 4 has the same shape (named victim, specific consequence, time anchor) just rendered in slower more clause-heavy prose.

---

## Beat 1 — The Hook (target 2,000 chars / first 30 seconds)

### Function
Pay off the title-thumbnail click within the first 30 seconds. Establish stakes. Name the threat. Promise a specific payoff. Embed at least three pattern interrupts (specific numbers, names, places) at 3s, 7s, 15s.

### Inside structure
Beat 1 has its own 4-part shape inside it:
1. **Anchor sentence** (first 5-7 seconds) — the specific scenario or claim that confirms the click was right
2. **Stakes amplifier** (5-15s) — why the audience specifically should care, with specific consequences
3. **Promise stack tease** (15-25s) — preview of the 5-promise stack (which becomes Beat 2)
4. **Open loop** (25-30s) — a specific question or wrinkle the script will return to

### Worked example
> "If you receive a Social Security retirement check, a Supplemental Security Income payment, a survivor benefit, or any monthly benefit administered by the Social Security Administration, the next four days are the most consequential four days of your year — and almost no retiree in America has been told what is about to happen. On April twenty third, the Treasury Department finalized a rule change that allows the Social Security Administration to redirect up to thirty seven percent of any monthly benefit, without a court order, without a hearing, and without your consent, to satisfy a debt the SSA itself decides you owe. The retirees most affected are not the ones with the largest debts. The retirees most affected are the ones who do not respond to a single specific letter that the SSA mails to an address on file that, in many cases, is more than a decade out of date. In the next twenty seven minutes, I am going to walk you through the four exact steps to verify whether your address on file is current, the seven dollar federal form that overrides the redirection automatically, the three categories of benefit that are exempt from the rule entirely, the specific phone tree path that gets you to a human SSA agent in under twelve minutes, and the one mistake that turns a recoverable redirection into a permanent loss of monthly income. There is one part of this rule that even the AARP got wrong in their public commentary. I will explain that part later in the video, because the way they got it wrong is precisely the way most retirees will get it wrong."

### Common failure modes
- **Generic opener.** "Hey guys, in today's video..." — nuke on sight.
- **Title gap.** The hook does not pay off the title's promise. The viewer feels baited.
- **Vague stakes.** "This could affect a lot of people" instead of "this affects an estimated forty seven million American retirees."
- **No pattern interrupts.** First 30 seconds without a specific number, name, or place. Retention dies.
- **Promise stack is generic.** "I'll show you 5 tips" instead of "Promise three is the seven dollar federal form that overrides the redirection automatically."
- **No open loop.** Beat 1 closes cleanly. Viewer feels free to leave.

### Self-check questions
- Is there a specific number, name, or place at 0:03? At 0:07? At 0:15?
- Does the audience-defining condition (first sentence) feel personal enough that the right viewer cannot leave?
- Are the 5 promises specific enough that each one carries its own tease?
- Is there at least one open loop the viewer must stay for?

## Beat 2 — The 5-Promise Stack (target 1,000 chars)

### Function
Make a contract with the viewer. Specifically, list the five things the video will deliver. The viewer commits to staying because each promise is specific enough that they cannot find it anywhere else in 30 seconds of scrolling.

### Inside structure
- One short setup sentence ("Here is exactly what I am going to walk you through.")
- Five numbered or sequenced promises, each ~150 chars
- Each promise references a specific number, form, dollar figure, or named action — never a vague benefit
- Each promise is delivered later in the video in beats 4-8

### Worked example
> "Here is exactly what I am going to walk you through. Promise number one is the four step verification that confirms whether your Social Security address on file is current, which you can do in under nine minutes from your kitchen table. Promise number two is the seven dollar federal form, Form SSA seven thousand four, that overrides the new redirection rule automatically when filed within the thirty day window. Promise number three is the three categories of benefit that are exempt from the rule entirely, including one category that the SSA representatives themselves frequently get wrong on the phone. Promise number four is the specific phone tree path that gets you to a human SSA agent in under twelve minutes, instead of the average forty one minute hold time most retirees experience. And promise number five is the single mistake that turns a recoverable redirection into a permanent loss of monthly income, which I see happen to roughly one in fifteen retirees who reach out to my office every quarter."

### Common failure modes
- **Vague promises.** "I'll explain how the rule works" — useless. The viewer cannot tell whether the explanation will be worth their time.
- **Numbered list with no anchors.** "First, we'll talk about the rule. Second, we'll discuss its impact." — read as filler.
- **Promises that don't pay off.** Promise four is teased but never delivered. The viewer leaves a comment about it. Trust score drops.
- **Stack too long.** Seven or eight promises dilute. Five is the discipline.
- **Stack at the wrong position.** If the promise stack lands before the hook is paid off, the viewer hasn't earned it yet and leaves.

### Self-check questions
- Does each promise contain at least one specific anchor (number, form, dollar amount, named outcome)?
- Are all five promises actually delivered later? (Track them through to beat 7.)
- Could the viewer find any of these promises in 30 seconds of competitor scrolling? If yes, the promise is not specific enough.

## Beat 3 — Credential Intro (target 300 chars)

### Function
Establish the voice's identity and credibility in one paragraph. Buy the right to deliver the next 24 minutes of authority. Never claim years of experience (avatar reads young).

### Inside structure
- One sentence: who is speaking, what role they hold, what they do
- One sentence: the specific stake or perspective that gives them this view
- Optional: a specific volume or scale claim ("the office handles roughly nine hundred filings a year")
- No life story. No origin story. The viewer is here for the threat in beat 1, not the speaker.

### Worked example
> "I work as a federal tax accountant. My office focuses on retirement income filings, Social Security overpayment disputes, and Medicare-adjacent benefit recoveries. The office handles roughly nine hundred filings a year, which is to say I see the same patterns repeat across the same demographic categories, season after season, and the rule we are discussing today is going to affect those patterns immediately."

### Common failure modes
- **Years claim.** "After fifteen years in the field..." — banned in this register because the avatar reads ~30.
- **Origin story.** "I started in this field because my grandmother..." — wastes the beat.
- **Vague title.** "I'm a financial expert" — every YouTube charlatan uses this. Be specific: "federal tax accountant focused on retirement income filings."
- **No specific stake.** "I just thought this was important." — gives no reason for the viewer to defer.
- **Long.** Anything over 350 chars dilutes the beat. The viewer is waiting for the payoff.

### Self-check questions
- Can the viewer summarize the speaker's credibility in one sentence after this beat?
- Is there a specific scale or volume claim that anchors the credibility?
- Is the beat shorter than 350 chars?

## Beat 4 — The Core Story / Concept (target 3,500 chars)

### Function
Deliver the founding case study or foundational concept. This is the longest single beat and the most important for the 30% retention valley. The viewer either commits here or leaves at minute 7-8.

### Inside structure
- **Patient/victim/case introduction** — name, age, location, situation in 2-3 sentences
- **The setup** — what was normal, what they expected, what was working
- **The trigger** — the specific event that created the situation
- **The unfolding** — what happened next, in concrete detail with dates, dollar amounts, named institutions
- **The current state** — where the story sits now, with stakes
- **The pivot** — one sentence that moves the script from "this happened to one person" to "this is happening to your demographic"

### Worked example
> "Patricia is seventy two years old. She lives in a small two-bedroom condo in Cincinnati that she bought with her late husband in nineteen ninety eight, paid off in twenty fifteen, and has been her sole residence ever since. She receives a Social Security retirement benefit of two thousand four hundred and ninety dollars per month, which is approximately ninety four percent of her total monthly income. The remaining six percent comes from a small pension from a textile company that closed in two thousand and seven.
>
> On March eleventh of this year, Patricia walked to her mailbox at the end of her driveway and retrieved a single piece of mail from the Social Security Administration. The letter was three pages long. The first page was a notice. The second page was a worksheet. The third page was a return envelope. Patricia read the first paragraph of the first page, did not understand what was being asked of her, set the letter on her kitchen counter, and went to the grocery store.
>
> Patricia did not respond to the letter. Patricia did not call the number on the letter. Patricia did not check the website on the letter. The letter sat on her kitchen counter for eighteen days, and on March twenty ninth, Patricia received her monthly benefit deposit. The deposit was for one thousand five hundred and sixty nine dollars. Nine hundred and twenty one dollars had been redirected without her signature, without a court order, and without a hearing.
>
> When Patricia called my office, the redirection had already taken effect for two consecutive months. She had been unable to pay her property taxes. She had been unable to refill a prescription. She had borrowed three hundred dollars from her sister in Toledo to cover groceries. The total amount she had lost, by the time she reached us, was one thousand eight hundred and forty two dollars.
>
> The reason I am opening with Patricia's story is not because Patricia is unusual. The reason I am opening with Patricia's story is because Patricia is exactly typical of the retiree most affected by this rule, and the single decision Patricia made — setting the letter on the counter and going to the grocery store — is the same decision that an estimated nine point three million American retirees are about to make in the next four days."

### Common failure modes
- **No name.** "A retiree in Ohio..." — abstract. Use a specific composite name.
- **No dollar amounts.** "She lost a lot of money" — gives the brain nothing to grip.
- **Story without consequence.** The patient's life doesn't change in a measurable way. The story is a vignette, not a stakes-engine.
- **No pivot.** The beat ends with "and that's Patricia's story" but never connects to the audience's life.
- **Padding.** Beat 4 should be the longest, but every sentence has to earn its space. No descriptive flourish.
- **Demographic mismatch.** Patricia is 35 in the example but the channel's audience is 65+. The viewer can't see themselves.

### Self-check questions
- Does the patient/victim have a specific name, age, location?
- Are there at least three specific dollar figures or measurable losses in the beat?
- Does the beat end with a one-sentence pivot from "this person" to "your demographic"?
- Could the viewer summarize the story in one sentence after watching?

## Beat 5 — The Mechanism Explained (target 3,500 chars)

### Function
Explain why the thing in beat 4 happens, in plain language the audience can repeat. This is the "now I understand" beat. Mechanism understanding is what creates the share-with-a-friend reflex later.

### Inside structure
- **One-sentence summary** — the mechanism in plain English
- **The legal/structural origin** — where the rule came from, when, and why
- **The named players** — the agency, the law, the form, the threshold
- **The how-it-actually-works walk-through** — step by step, in the order the system applies them
- **The hidden detail** — the part the news coverage didn't cover, which is what makes the channel valuable
- **The anchor back to beat 4** — restate what happened to Patricia in light of the mechanism

### Worked example
> "The mechanism behind what happened to Patricia is, in plain English, this. The Treasury Department, working through the Social Security Administration, has been authorized to recover overpayment debts directly from monthly benefits, without a court order, when the SSA's internal records indicate that a beneficiary owes a debt to the federal government. The authorization is not new. The authorization has existed in some form since nineteen ninety six. What is new is the threshold. The threshold for triggering the redirection used to be debts over five thousand dollars. As of April first, the threshold has been lowered to one hundred dollars.
>
> What this means in practice is that any beneficiary whose internal SSA file contains any flag of any debt over one hundred dollars — including debts that were assessed in error, debts that were paid but not recorded, debts that were the result of a clerical mistake, or debts that the beneficiary has actively been disputing — is now eligible for the same redirection that Patricia experienced.
>
> The rule applies in the following exact sequence. First, the SSA's internal accounting system flags the debt. Second, the system generates a single notice letter to the address on file. Third, the system grants the beneficiary a thirty day window to respond. Fourth, if the beneficiary does not respond within thirty days — and only if they do not respond — the system automatically redirects up to thirty seven percent of the next month's benefit toward the debt. The redirection continues until the debt is satisfied or the beneficiary files Form SSA seven thousand four to formally dispute it.
>
> Here is the part that almost no public commentary on the rule has covered. The thirty day window does not begin when the beneficiary receives the letter. The thirty day window begins on the date the letter is mailed, which is printed in the upper right corner of page one. For a beneficiary whose address on file is out of date, the letter may take ten or fifteen days to forward. By the time the letter arrives, the beneficiary has fifteen or twenty days to act, not thirty. For a beneficiary in a senior care facility where mail is sorted weekly, the window can collapse to under a week.
>
> Patricia's letter was mailed on March eleventh. By the time Patricia walked to her mailbox, retrieved the letter, and set it on her kitchen counter, she had already used four days of her thirty day window. By the time her benefit deposited in late March, she had used twenty days. By the time she called my office, she had no window left. The mechanism worked exactly as designed. Patricia is not unusual. Patricia is the design target."

### Common failure modes
- **Jargon dump.** Unloading the legal language without translating it.
- **No origin.** Explaining the rule without naming when it was created or by whom.
- **No hidden detail.** If the mechanism explanation reads like the news coverage, the channel adds no value.
- **No anchor back to beat 4.** The mechanism is explained in the abstract and the patient story disappears.
- **Wrong granularity.** Either too vague (single paragraph summary) or too deep (15 paragraphs of legal text). The target is "viewer can re-explain it to a friend at dinner."

### Self-check questions
- Could the viewer explain this mechanism to a friend in three sentences?
- Is there at least one specific detail that did not appear in the news coverage of this rule?
- Does the beat end by re-anchoring to the patient story from beat 4?

## Beat 6 — Three Detailed Examples (target 4,500 chars)

### Function
Apply the mechanism from beat 5 to three more composite cases. Vary demographics so different segments of the audience see themselves. This is the "I am Patricia, but also I am Robert, but also I am Joyce" beat.

### Inside structure
- **Example 1** — typical case, demographics like Patricia. Reinforces the pattern.
- **Example 2** — edge case, different demographics. Expands the affected population.
- **Example 3** — counter-intuitive case, the one that surprises. Shows the rule's full reach.
- Each example: name, age, location, trigger, consequence, dollar amount, current state. ~1,500 chars each.
- Examples are clearly composites (no real PII), but feel real because the demographic and dollar specifics are calibrated.

### Worked example (Example 1 only — pattern repeats for 2 and 3)
> "Robert is sixty seven years old. He lives in a manufactured home community outside Phoenix. He receives a Social Security retirement benefit of two thousand one hundred and thirteen dollars per month, plus a small Veterans Affairs disability check of three hundred and sixty seven dollars per month. Robert's address on file with the SSA is an address he has not lived at since two thousand and nineteen. The address is his late sister's house in Tucson. His sister passed away in twenty twenty one. The house has been vacant since then, and the mail is collected by a neighbor approximately once a month.
>
> On April second, the SSA mailed Robert a letter regarding a six hundred and forty dollar overpayment that the agency had assessed in two thousand and seventeen and that Robert had successfully disputed at the time. The dispute resolution was filed in Robert's paper file, which has not been digitized. The system flagged the debt as outstanding. The letter went to Tucson. By the time Robert's neighbor collected the mail and forwarded it to Phoenix, the thirty day window had closed. Robert's May benefit was deposited at one thousand four hundred and eighty seven dollars. Robert had lost six hundred and twenty six dollars to a debt he did not owe.
>
> Robert is the case that demonstrates the address-on-file failure. The rule, as written, presumes that the address on file is current. For Robert, and for an estimated three point seven million beneficiaries with an out-of-date address, the presumption is wrong, and the cost of the wrong presumption is borne entirely by the beneficiary."

### Common failure modes
- **Same demographics three times.** All three examples are 70-year-old women in the Midwest. The audience segments outside that don't see themselves.
- **No demographic variation.** Robert, Patricia, and Joyce are all retired teachers. Vary occupations, regions, life situations.
- **Examples are too similar to beat 4.** The third example should reveal a NEW dimension of the rule, not repeat the first.
- **Examples without dollar amounts.** Vague "lost a lot of money" reads as fiction.
- **Real PII.** Even by accident. Always composite.
- **Length collapse.** Three examples at 800 chars each is too thin. 1,500 chars each is the discipline.

### Self-check questions
- Do the three examples vary in demographics (age, region, occupation, life situation)?
- Does each example expose a different facet of the mechanism?
- Are dollar amounts and dates specific in each example?
- Could three different audience segments each say "that's me"?

## Beat 7 — The Numbered List (target 5,000 chars, 5-7 items)

### Function
Deliver the actionable payoff that beats 1, 2, 4, 5, 6 set up. Each item is a specific protective action the viewer can take this week. This beat resolves the promise stack from beat 2.

### Inside structure
- One short transition sentence ("Here is exactly what to do, in this order.")
- 5-7 numbered items, each ~700-1,000 chars
- Each item: title (one short sentence), explanation (3-4 sentences), specific actionable step (form number, phone number, URL, dollar amount, time anchor)
- Items are sequenced by urgency — most time-sensitive first
- Each item closes a specific promise from beat 2

### Worked example (Items 1 and 2 only — pattern repeats for items 3-5)
> "Here is exactly what to do, in this order, starting today.
>
> Item one. Verify your address on file with the Social Security Administration. The fastest way to do this is to log in to your my Social Security account at SSA dot gov, click the My Profile tab, and read the address listed under Mailing Address. If the address shown is not the address you currently live at, click Update Profile and enter the correct address. The system will email you a confirmation, and the change is effective within seventy two hours. If you do not have an online SSA account, the verification can be completed in person at any SSA field office, with two forms of identification, in approximately twenty minutes. Do this within the next four days. Every day your address is wrong is a day the thirty day response window collapses against you.
>
> Item two. File Form SSA seven thousand four to override the redirection rule preemptively. The form is one page. The form is free. The form is available at SSA dot gov forward slash forms forward slash SSA dash seven oh oh four dot pdf, or in person at any field office, or by calling one eight hundred seven seven two one two one three and asking for the form to be mailed. Once filed, the form remains active for twenty four months and prevents any redirection of your monthly benefit during that window for any debt under five thousand dollars. The form does not eliminate the debt, if a debt exists. The form simply requires that the debt be resolved through a hearing before any redirection occurs. For ninety four percent of disputed debts, the hearing reveals the debt was assessed in error and is dismissed. The seven dollar fee referenced in some news coverage is a fee for expedited processing. The form itself is free."

### Common failure modes
- **Items without specifics.** "Make sure your address is current" — gives the viewer nothing to actually do.
- **Items in the wrong sequence.** Most-important last, time-sensitive deep in the middle. Sequence by urgency.
- **Items overlap.** Two items say the same thing in different words. Each item must be distinct.
- **Items too short.** A 200-char item gets read as throwaway. 700-1,000 chars per item creates depth.
- **Promise stack mismatch.** Beat 2 promised five things; beat 7 delivers six items, one of which has no promise. Or beat 2 promised five things; beat 7 covers four. Audit alignment before locking.
- **No URL/form/number.** Each item must be actionable. If the viewer cannot act on it within seven days, it does not belong.

### Self-check questions
- Does each item start with a specific action verb?
- Does each item contain at least one URL, form number, or phone number?
- Are items sequenced by urgency, with the most time-sensitive first?
- Does each item close exactly one promise from beat 2?

## Beat 8 — Bonus / Advanced Move (target 1,500 chars)

### Function
Sticks the 80% retention valley. Re-engages skim viewers. Positions the channel as the place that goes deeper than the news. Often the most-shared single sentence in the script.

### Inside structure
- **Setup line** — "Almost no one is using this..." or "There is one move that even most CPAs are not aware of..."
- **The move itself** — specific, one paragraph, with a named lever
- **Why it works** — the mechanism in one or two sentences
- **The caveat** — when not to use it, who it does not apply to
- **The action** — exactly how to do it

### Worked example
> "There is one move that the news coverage of this rule has not mentioned and that even most senior accountants do not yet know about. If you have any debt flagged in your SSA file from before two thousand and ten — any debt at all, regardless of amount — you can request a Statute of Limitations Review using IRS Form CP fifty two. The form invokes a federal statute of limitations on collection that, in most states, sets a maximum recovery window of ten years from the date of original assessment. For debts older than ten years, the SSA is statutorily barred from redirecting benefits, regardless of whether the debt was ever resolved on paper. The form is one page. It is free. The processing window is approximately sixty days. For roughly thirty one percent of the retirees in my office's caseload, this single form has cleared a debt the SSA had been carrying as outstanding for over a decade. If your file shows any debt with an original assessment date before two thousand fifteen, this is the move. If your debts are all recent, this does not apply to you. If you are unsure, request a free Annual Earnings Statement from the SSA, which lists every assessed debt with its original date."

### Common failure modes
- **Vague advanced move.** "Talk to a professional about your situation." — useless. Be specific.
- **Move that contradicts beat 7.** Item 5 says X; bonus says do Y. Inconsistency erodes trust.
- **Move that doesn't apply broadly.** If only 0.1% of viewers can use it, it's not a bonus, it's an aside.
- **No caveat.** Every advanced move has a "when not to use" clause. Without it, the move sounds like a get-rich-quick.
- **Setup line without payoff.** "Here's the move almost no one knows about — make sure to consult an attorney." Banned.

### Self-check questions
- Does the move have a named form, statute, or specific lever?
- Does the beat include a clear "when not to use" caveat?
- Could a viewer execute the move within 14 days of watching?
- Does the move feel different in kind from the items in beat 7, not just one more item?

## Beat 9 — Lead Magnet CTA (target 1,000 chars)

### Function
Convert watchers to email subscribers using a free guide that delivers more depth than the video. The lead magnet is the channel's owned audience asset.

### Inside structure
- **Specific lead magnet name** — never generic
- **What's in it** — 3-5 specific things the magnet contains
- **Specific URL** — short, brandable, mentioned twice (early and late in beat)
- **Why it matters** — one sentence on the gap the magnet fills

### Worked example
> "I have written a free guide called the Retiree Protection Checklist, which I am giving away at MarkusGravesTax dot com slash checklist. The checklist is forty seven pages. It contains every form number I referenced in this video, with direct links to the federal source. It contains the exact phone tree path to reach an SSA agent in under twelve minutes. It contains the seventeen most common debt categories my office sees disputed and the form needed to dispute each one. And it contains a one page printable summary that you can keep on your refrigerator or share with the family member who handles your finances. The guide is free. There is no upsell. The link is MarkusGravesTax dot com slash checklist, and I will put it in the description below."

### Common failure modes
- **Generic name.** "Download my free guide." — what guide? Specific name.
- **Vague contents.** "It has everything you need." — what specifically?
- **Long URL.** Anything the viewer can't memorize for 30 seconds is lost.
- **Upsell tease.** "And if you want even more, my course..." — breaks trust at the moment of trust-building.

### Self-check questions
- Does the lead magnet have a specific name?
- Are at least three specific contents listed?
- Is the URL short enough to remember?
- Is the magnet free with no upsell mentioned?

## Beat 10 — Affiliate Pitch (target 500 chars)

### Function
Generate revenue without breaking voice. One product, brief, disclosure-clean.

### Inside structure
- **Brief context** — one sentence on why this product is mentioned now
- **The product** — named, with specific use case
- **The disclosure** — brief, FTC-compliant, in-voice
- **The link** — in description, mentioned briefly

### Worked example
> "Many of you will need to file an amended return after running through the items in this video. The software my office uses for amended retiree returns is a major tax-software affiliate, and they have set up a discount code for retirees in this audience. The code gives twenty five percent off the federal filing tier. I am an affiliate, which means I earn a small commission if you use the code. The link is in the description."

### Common failure modes
- **Hard sell.** "You absolutely must buy this." — banned. The voice softens, doesn't push.
- **No disclosure.** FTC violation. Always disclose.
- **Two products.** One product per video. More dilutes both.
- **Affiliate before lead magnet.** Lead magnet always comes first; affiliate after.
- **Affiliate that contradicts the video.** Don't sell a course on the topic the video just covered for free.

### Self-check questions
- Is exactly one product named?
- Is the disclosure clear and in-voice?
- Does the affiliate fit the audience demographic (no SaaS pitches to retirees)?

## Beat 11 — Close + Like/Subscribe (target 1,500 chars)

### Function
Restate the stakes. Call the viewer to act. Trigger like/subscribe behavior. Reinforce voice for the repeat viewer.

### Inside structure
- **Stakes restate** — one paragraph that re-anchors the threat from beat 1
- **Action prompt** — what to do this week
- **Voice signature** — the channel's signature close phrase
- **Like/subscribe** — embedded naturally, not bolted on
- **Optional callback** — to a future video or recurring segment

### Worked example
> "If you take nothing else from this video, take this. The single decision that determines whether you keep your full Social Security benefit through the next year is whether your address on file is current and whether Form SSA seven thousand four is on file with the agency. The decision takes nine minutes. The decision is free. And the decision protects you from a redirection that an estimated nine point three million American retirees are about to walk into without warning.
>
> If this video helped you, the most useful thing you can do is hit the like button and subscribe to the channel. Every subscriber helps the algorithm show this video to one more retiree who needs it. And if you know a friend, a parent, a neighbor who receives a Social Security benefit, send them the link in the description. The next video on this channel covers the three Medicare changes that take effect in May, and the one I want you to know about most is the one that affects retirees with any preexisting cardiac condition."

### Common failure modes
- **Generic close.** "Thanks for watching, and I'll see you in the next video." — wastes the beat.
- **Like-and-subscribe bolted on.** Reads as performative. Embed in the stakes restatement.
- **No future hook.** The next video is not previewed; the channel loses the next-click.
- **Restating without anchoring.** Restate the stakes WITH a specific number from the video. Vague restates don't land.

### Self-check questions
- Does the close restate the stakes with a specific anchor from earlier in the video?
- Is the like/subscribe embedded naturally?
- Is there a specific preview of the next video?
- Does the beat sound like the same voice that opened the script?

## Beat 12 — Share Prompt (target 500 chars)

### Function
Convert one watcher into multiple watchers. The share prompt is the highest-leverage 30 seconds in the script for view growth.

### Inside structure
- **Specific share recipient** — "send this to a friend who..." not "share with everyone"
- **Specific reason** — why this person specifically should see it
- **Channel signature close phrase** — the locked-in phrase the channel always closes with

### Worked example
> "If you have a parent, a grandparent, or a neighbor who receives any monthly benefit from the Social Security Administration, send them this video. The four days between today and the rule taking effect are the only window they have to file Form SSA seven thousand four preemptively, and most of them have not been told. I will see you in the next video."

### Common failure modes
- **Generic share.** "Share with anyone you think might benefit." — does not trigger sharing.
- **No urgency.** Without a time anchor, the viewer doesn't share now.
- **Wrong signature close.** Every personal-finance authority channel video closes with "I will see you in the next video." Channel signatures are sacrosanct.

### Self-check questions
- Is there a specific recipient described?
- Is there a specific reason the recipient should see it now?
- Does the beat end with the channel's locked signature close?

## Cross-beat self-check

After all 12 beats are drafted, the agent runs a final pass:

1. **Promise audit.** Beat 2 promised five things. Beat 7 delivers all five. Walk through each.
2. **Open loop audit.** Beat 1 opened a loop ("the AARP got it wrong"). Beat 5 or 6 resolved it.

## How to re-skin this for other voices

The beat shapes are voice-agnostic. To re-skin for a different archetype:

- **V1 Documentary Authority (long-form mystery-documentary / science-explainer register):** Replace named patient with named historical figure. Replace dollar amounts with measurements/dates. Replace forms with primary sources. Slow the rhythm. Add clauses.
- **V4 Skeptical Investigator (investigative-journalism register):** Replace patient story with the named villain's claim. Replace mechanism with the documents that disprove the claim. Replace numbered list with the evidence chain.
- **V6 Hype Showman (top viral-challenge register):** Compress beats 4-6 into one rolling escalation. Replace mechanism with the scale/budget reveal. Replace numbered list with the contestant elimination sequence.
- **V8 Erudite Professor (economic-explainer register):** Replace named patient with a named institution. Replace dollar amounts with macro figures (billions, percentages of GDP). Replace numbered list with consequences across multiple sectors.
- **V13 Reluctant Witness (true crime):** Replace mechanism with the timeline of events. Replace numbered list with the unanswered questions. Replace bonus advanced move with the new evidence the channel uncovered.

The agent always picks voice first, then applies these beat shapes through that voice.

## When to consult this companion

The agent loads this file when:
- Writing the deep middle of a script (beats 4-7)
- Self-checking a draft for retention failure points
- Adapting the 12-beat structure to a new niche
- Teaching a user why their previous channel's scripts collapsed at the 30% or 60% mark
- Building memory rules from feedback that affects beat shape (rather than voice)

The agent does not load this file when:
- The niche uses a non-12-beat structural variant (vertical shorts, music video, ambient loop) — those have their own shapes in Skill 03 §4

- The voice DNA is the open question, not the structure — go to Skill 03 §3 instead
