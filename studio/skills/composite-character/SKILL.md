# Tactical Playbook T3 — Composite Character Generation

This is the master playbook for inventing **composite characters** — fictional people built from real patterns who serve as the protagonist or victim of a script's story arc. Used heavily in finance, health, scam-exposure, and news-hijack content where every script anchors on a specific human story.

The single rule: **a composite must feel like a real person, not a "category."** "Margaret, 67, retired teacher from Cleveland" works. "An average retiree" doesn't. The audience identifies with specifics; specifics drive retention; vague composites collapse into noise.

This playbook covers when to use composites, how to build them, how to render them visually, and how to lock them into channel memory so they recur naturally across episodes.

---

## 1. The job of a composite character

Every story-driven script needs a protagonist or victim. Three options for who that person is:

**Option A — Real named subject** (e.g., a composite styled after a real publicly-documented case, like "Helen Margate, the Midwest restaurant owner whose money was seized by the IRS"). Strongest for credibility but limited to publicly-documented cases — and even then, use a composite name unless the script specifically requires the real identity.

**Option B — Anonymized real subject** ("a woman in Iowa, who we'll call Marilyn for this story"). Stronger than full composite, weaker than named real subject. Good for legally-sensitive stories.

**Option C — Composite character** ("Margaret, 67, a retired teacher in Cleveland"). Built from real patterns but not a single real person. Best for content where the situation is universal but specific cases are private/protected.

Most authority channels use Option C 70-80% of the time. Composites give the script:
- **Permission to specify details** without legal exposure
- **Demographic targeting** to match audience
- **Narrative flexibility** to construct the exact arc the script needs
- **Recurring characters** that audiences come to recognize across episodes

The agent's job is to build composites that feel real, are demographically appropriate, are visually consistent, and are stored in channel memory for reuse.

## 2. When composites are right vs wrong

### Use composites when:
- The story illustrates a universal pattern (every retiree faces this; every cancer patient encounters this)
- Real cases exist but are legally protected (HIPAA, sealed records, ongoing litigation)
- The script needs specific details that no single real case happens to provide
- The audience would identify more with a constructed-everyman than a single celebrity case
- You're protecting vulnerable people whose stories you've heard but don't have permission to share publicly

### Use real named subjects when:
- The case is publicly documented (court records, journalism, public testimony)
- The story IS the case (you're telling THIS specific person's story)
- Authority comes from naming the person
- The Institute for Justice, ProPublica, or similar source has already published the case

### Use anonymized real subjects when:
- A specific person told you their story but doesn't want their name used
- The case has identifying details that protect the source

The agent always asks the user if a real-named source exists before defaulting to composite. Real cases are stronger when available.

## 3. The character matrix

When the agent invents a composite, it fills this matrix:

| Variable | What it controls | Default thinking |
|---|---|---|
| **Name** | Reader recognition + memorability | First name only, period-appropriate, demographic-appropriate |
| **Age** | Audience identification | Match the channel's primary audience age (or 5-10 years older for "wise" register) |
| **Gender** | Audience identification | Match channel demographic split (senior finance: 60% female; tech: 70% male) |
| **Location** | Specificity + audience identification | Specific city + state. Mid-sized cities feel more real than NYC/LA |
| **Occupation (current or former)** | Backstory anchor + credibility | Match the situation. Retired teacher for finance content. Truck driver for blue-collar content. |
| **Family situation** | Stakes + emotional weight | Spouse status, children, grandchildren — but don't overload |
| **Income / asset profile** | Story-relevant detail | Specific dollar amounts in some cases; "comfortable retirement" in others |
| **Specific defining trait** | Memorability | One unusual specific detail that makes them feel real |
| **Emotional register** | How they should be visualized | Calm / weary / determined / confused / scared / triumphant |
| **Visual archetype** | T1 host playbook reference | Match composite to a profession/setting reference |

### Worked composite — Margaret

User says: "I need a composite for a story about a retired teacher who almost got scammed by a phishing email pretending to be from her bank."

Agent fills matrix:
- **Name:** Margaret (period-appropriate for someone now 67)
- **Age:** 67
- **Gender:** Female (matches senior finance / scam awareness audience)
- **Location:** Cleveland, Ohio (mid-sized, relatable, not coastal)
- **Occupation:** Retired elementary school teacher, 34 years
- **Family:** Widowed three years ago, two adult children, four grandchildren
- **Income:** $2,600/month Social Security + $900/month state pension
- **Defining trait:** Still uses email address she made when she first got internet in 1999; her grandchildren tease her about her AOL account
- **Emotional register:** Wary but trusting — she's seen scams before but this email looked exactly like her bank
- **Visual archetype:** Retired teacher per T1 matrix (cardigan, kitchen table, soft window light, age 65-72)

The script can now reference Margaret naturally throughout. She has texture. She has stakes. She has a specific email address that her grandchildren tease her about — a detail that takes 4 seconds to mention but makes her real.

## 4. The naming conventions

Composite names matter more than they seem. Period and demographic appropriateness drives believability.

### For senior demographics (current age 60-85)
Names that were common when this generation was born:
- **Female:** Margaret, Linda, Patricia, Joan, Carol, Susan, Helen, Frances, Dorothy, Barbara, Judith, Karen, Deborah, Nancy, Janet, Catherine, Ruth, Joyce
- **Male:** Robert, James, Tom (Thomas), Frank, Walter, Richard, William, Donald, Charles, Harold, George, Ronald, Edward, Kenneth, Paul, Henry

### For middle-aged demographics (current age 40-60)
- **Female:** Jennifer, Lisa, Michelle, Stephanie, Kimberly, Rebecca, Heather, Tracy, Christina, Amy
- **Male:** Michael, David, John, Mark, Steven, Christopher, Brian, Eric, Gary, Daniel

### For younger demographics (current age 25-40)
- **Female:** Ashley, Emily, Sarah, Jessica, Megan, Lauren, Brittany, Hannah, Samantha, Madison
- **Male:** Tyler, Brandon, Justin, Joshua, Andrew, Matthew, Anthony, Kevin, Ryan, Jonathan

### Cross-period or modern names — caution
Names like "Aiden," "Mason," "Olivia" for adult composites read as "agent invented this name" because they're 2010s-2020s baby names. Don't use them for adult characters.

### Last names — avoid in most cases
Composites typically don't need last names. "Margaret in Cleveland" works. "Margaret Hinderly in Cleveland" feels invented. Use last names only when:
- The story specifically requires identification by both names
- The audience needs to differentiate two same-first-name composites in one script
- The composite has a real-named inspiration whose last name is being changed

### Recurring composites
Some channels develop a roster of recurring composites that audiences come to know:
- **Personal-finance authority channel:** Patricia (Chase scam victim), Linda (SSA overpayment), Helen (composite based on a public IRS forfeiture case), Robert and Joan (Roth conversion couple), Dave and Margaret (inherited IRA), Tom and Helen (estate planning)
- **Health channels:** typical recurring patient personas with specific conditions

The agent saves recurring composites to channel memory with their full character matrix so they can be referenced across episodes consistently.

## 5. The "specific defining trait" rule

The single thing that separates a believable composite from a generic one is the specific defining trait — a detail that isn't structurally necessary but anchors the character as a real person.

Examples of strong defining traits:
- "She still uses an AOL email address from 1999"
- "He keeps a paper checkbook ledger because he doesn't trust banking apps"
- "She has a daughter who lives 40 minutes away and visits every other Sunday"
- "He retired from the same company his father worked at, after 41 years"
- "She bought a Tesla last year against her better judgment and now she loves it"
- "He volunteers at the local VFW post Tuesdays and Thursdays"
- "She still drives the 2007 Honda Accord she bought when her son was in college"

Each of these takes 5-10 words to mention. Each one establishes a real person in the listener's brain instantly.

Avoid weak defining traits:
- "She loves spending time with her family" (vague, applies to everyone)
- "He's been through a lot in his life" (vague, applies to everyone)
- "She's a hard worker" (template phrase)

The agent invents one specific defining trait per composite.

## 6. The visual rendering protocol

Once the composite character matrix is filled, the agent renders the visual via T1 (AI Host Setup). The composite's visual matches:

1. **Profession** → T1 profession matrix row
2. **Age** → match to T1 age range for that profession
3. **Setting** → T2 (Authentic Setting Generation) appropriate to the story
4. **Emotional register** → guides the expression in the rendering
5. **Locked reference** → if recurring composite, save the rendered image to channel memory and reuse for any thumbnail / B-roll appearance

The composite's visual should be locked the FIRST time the composite is introduced and never re-rendered for subsequent episodes. Margaret in Episode 12 should look like Margaret in Episode 25.

## 7. Composite character bible — channel memory format

For recurring composites, the agent maintains a character bible in channel memory:

```

- Age: 67 (locked at creation; ages with channel)
- Gender: Female
- Location: Cleveland, Ohio
- Occupation: Retired elementary school teacher (34 years)
- Family: Widowed 3 years, 2 adult children, 4 grandchildren
- Income: $2,600/mo SS + $900/mo OH teacher pension
- Defining trait: AOL email address from 1999, grandkids tease her about it
- Emotional baseline: Wary but trusting

- T1 matrix row: Retired teacher
- T2 setting: Modest home study with cardigan, soft window light
- Locked image URL: [reference image stored in R2]
- Voice DNA pairing: V13 Reluctant Witness (composite victim register)

Episode appearances:
- Early episode — SSA overpayment story (introduced)
- Mid-run episode — Roth conversion (mentioned briefly)

```

This bible is loaded whenever Margaret is referenced in a future script. Her age may advance slightly across episodes (1-2 years over a year of content) but her core identity stays locked.

## 8. The "don't over-load" rule

Every composite should have:
- Name + age + location + occupation
- One family detail
- One financial detail
- One defining trait

Total: ~6 specific facts.

More than 8 facts and the composite becomes a list, not a person. Less than 4 and the composite reads as a category.

The agent never invents:
- Multiple specific defining traits per composite (one is enough)
- Detailed backstories spanning multiple decades unless specifically required
- Romantic histories or inter-family drama (out of scope for most authority content)
- Specific medical histories beyond what the script directly requires

Backstory expands on demand. The agent invents the matrix, then the script invents what it needs.

## 9. Demographic targeting rules

The composite's demographics should match the channel's audience demographic ±5 years.

**Senior finance / IRS / retirement (personal-finance authority channel):**
- Audience: 60-80, female-leaning (55%)
- Composites: 60-80, mostly female with male variants
- Names from senior demographic name list

**Tech / AI / dev:**
- Audience: 25-45, male-leaning (70%)
- Composites: 25-45, mostly male with female variants
- Names from middle/younger demographic name list

**Health / supplements (medical-authority channel):**
- Audience: 50-75, female-leaning (60%)
- Composites: 50-75, mix
- Names from senior or middle demographic list depending on age

**News-hijack documentary:**
- Audience varies — composite matches the specific case
- Variable demographics

The agent matches composite demographics to audience without requiring user instruction. If user says "make a composite for a story about scammed retirees," the agent doesn't need to ask demographics — defaults are clear from niche.

## 10. Anti-patterns

**A1 — Generic-everyone composite.** "A retiree faces this issue." No specifics. The audience doesn't identify; the script lacks anchor.

**A2 — Anachronistic name.** "Olivia, 73, retired in 1995" — Olivia wasn't a popular name in the 1940s when this composite would have been born. Period-mismatched names break realism.

**A3 — Overloaded backstory.** Composite has a paragraph of personal history. Reads as creative writing exercise, not authority script.

**A4 — Vague defining trait.** "She loves her family" applies to everyone. No anchor.

**A5 — Demographic mismatch.** Senior finance content with a 28-year-old composite. Audience identifies less.

**A6 — Last-name overuse.** "Margaret Hinderly Robertson, 67, retired from Cleveland Heights School District" — too much. Just Margaret.

**A7 — Visual drift.** Composite rendered differently in an early episode vs a later episode because the agent didn't lock the reference. Audience notices.

**A8 — Too-specific real-person details.** Composite that's clearly based on a real public person without acknowledgment. Legal risk + ethical risk.

**A9 — Multi-composite confusion in one script.** Three composites in one script with similar names (Margaret, Marilyn, Maureen). Audience can't track.

**A10 — Composite contradiction across episodes.** Margaret was a teacher in an early episode but a nurse in a later episode because the agent forgot to lock memory. Audience notices.

## 12. Sample-then-confirm gate

When introducing a NEW composite that will recur:
1. Agent fills character matrix
2. Surfaces matrix to user with the proposed name + key details
3. User approves OR adjusts
4. Once approved, character bible saved to channel memory
5. Visual rendered via T1 + saved as locked reference
6. All future episodes reference the locked bible + locked visual

For one-off composites in a single script:
1. Agent fills matrix during script generation
2. Surfaces script with composite embedded
3. User reads script, approves implicitly via script approval

Major characters (recurring) get explicit gate. Minor characters (one-script-only) ride with script approval.

## 13. Runtime checklist

Before any composite is introduced in a script:
- [ ] Real-named subject availability checked (composite is fallback, not default)
- [ ] Character matrix filled with all 10 variables
- [ ] Name period-appropriate to age
- [ ] Demographic-aligned to channel audience
- [ ] One specific defining trait invented
- [ ] Visual archetype matched (T1 + T2)
- [ ] If recurring: explicit user approval, character bible saved
- [ ] If one-off: integrated into script, surfaced for review
- [ ] Cross-episode consistency check (existing composites not contradicted)

## 14. Why these rules

The audience for story-driven authority content (finance, health, true crime, news-hijack) processes character information faster than they process abstract claims. A specific named composite with a defining trait lands in the listener's brain in ~3 seconds and stays there. An abstract category ("a retiree facing X") never lands at all.

Composites also let the channel develop a recurring cast over time. A personal-finance authority channel's Patricia is a recurring composite that the audience recognizes — she's not a real person, but she carries the brand's "victim of bank surveillance" archetype. Audiences who've watched 5+ of the channel's videos start anticipating Patricia (or her variants), which deepens engagement.

The key is **never let composites collapse into stereotypes.** A specific defining trait per composite, demographic appropriateness, period-correct name, locked visual reference, and explicit channel memory tracking — that's the discipline that keeps the cast feeling real.
