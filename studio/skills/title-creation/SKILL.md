# Skill 01 — Title Creation

This is the operational knowledge an AI YouTube agent needs to produce the perfect title for any video in any niche. Every paragraph is a concrete rule the agent can cite and apply.

---

## 1. The job of a title

A title is a contract. The viewer reads it, predicts what they'll get, and clicks if the predicted payoff exceeds their alternatives in that 30-second feed-scrolling moment. Every title makes a promise. Every video has to pay that promise off within the first 30 seconds or watch-time collapses and the algorithm punishes the next upload.

The agent's job is to produce a title that:
- **Wins the click** against ~12 competing thumbnails on a mobile screen
- **Previews the satisfaction** the video delivers (a clickbait-only title hurts the channel within 24 hours)
- **Signals to the algorithm** what topic and audience this video is for, so it gets surfaced to the right cohort

The agent never optimizes for click-through rate alone. The 2025-2026 algorithm judges every video on **CTR × AVD × Satisfaction**. A 20% CTR with 30% retention loses to a 7% CTR with 65% retention.

Quote from YouTube's Senior Director of Growth & Discovery, Todd Beaupre: *"If we showed everyone the most clickable thumbnail and got 20% CTR but the video only delivered for 5% of viewers, we'd be optimizing for the wrong thing."* This is the single most important sentence in title strategy as of 2025.

## 2. The optimization target

The agent optimizes titles against **predicted watch-time-per-impression**, not raw CTR. This is the metric YouTube uses internally and the metric YouTube's native Test & Compare feature uses to pick the winner of an A/B test.

Predicted watch-time-per-impression for a candidate title is a function of:
- **CTR potential** — does the title win the click against the niche median?
- **Hook alignment** — does the title set up a payoff the script's first 30 seconds can deliver?
- **Audience fit** — does the title attract the cohort that retains, not the cohort that bounces?
- **Niche pattern fit** — does the title match what works in this specific niche?

A title that nails all four metrics is "watch-time positive." A title that nails only CTR is "click-trap" and burns the channel.

The agent's mental model: "Will this title generate 8 minutes of watched content per 100 impressions?" Not "will this title generate 12 clicks per 100 impressions?"

## 3. The 13 canonical title formulas

Every winning title in 2025-2026 fits one of these 13 formulas, or combines two of them. The agent generates 12 variants per video using a spread across formulas, then ranks by predicted watch-time-per-impression.

### F1 — Em-Dash Mystery Gap

**Structure:** `[Specific concrete claim] — [Mystery payoff at 75-85% position]`

**Examples:**
- *Why Banks Fear Customers Who Keep $20,000 in Cash — The Account Clause Nobody Reads*
- *$160,000 Gone In One Phone Call — Why Chase Won't Give It Back*
- *He Built A $40M Business — Then Lost It In 90 Days*

**When to use:** Any niche with a story arc or a hidden mechanism. Strongest for finance, true crime, history, science.

**When NOT to use:** Pure how-to / educational tutorials where the payoff has to be in the title for SEO.

**Why it works:** The em-dash creates a curiosity gap. The first half makes a concrete promise. The second half teases a hidden answer. Mobile feed truncation tests show the em-dash often falls at the visibility cutoff, which forces the user to click to see the resolution.

**Expected lift:** Em-dash titles outperform colon titles by approximately 10-15% CTR in the same A/B condition (Spotter Studio data, 2024).

### F2 — Specific Dollar Number

**Structure:** `[$X amount] [verb] [outcome] — [optional mystery]`

**Examples:**
- *$246,000 Gone By Filing Social Security At 70 (Most Retirees Don't Know This)*
- *I Spent $1M On A Single Trade — Here's What Happened*
- *$32,820 Seized. Zero Charges Filed.*

**When to use:** Finance, business, real estate, retirement, lifestyle income claims. Works in any niche where a dollar figure conveys stakes.

**When NOT to use:** Niches where money discussion feels off-brand (faith, parenting, comedy).

**Why it works:** Specific dollar numbers are processed differently by the brain than vague qualifiers. "$32,820" is more click-worthy than "thousands of dollars" because the specificity signals authority and concreteness. Spotter Studio data: numbered titles outperform unnumbered titles by 36% on average.

**Critical rule:** Use odd, specific, "discovered" numbers (7, 13, 23, 47) over round, "manufactured" numbers (10, 20, 50). Odd numbers feel real. Round numbers feel marketed.

### F3 — Counter-Narrative

**Structure:** `[Conventional wisdom statement] — [Why it's wrong] / [Numbered reasons]`

**Examples:**
- *These 7 REASONS Will Make You File Social Security at Age 62 (NOT 70)*
- *Stop Investing In Index Funds — Do This Instead*
- *Why "Drink 8 Glasses of Water" Is Wrong*

**When to use:** Any niche where there's a dominant "everyone says X" assumption you can credibly subvert. Especially powerful when you have credentials.

**When NOT to use:** When you can't actually subvert the wisdom credibly. The video has to deliver. A counter-narrative that doesn't pay off creates immediate dislikes.

**Why it works:** The brain is wired to attend to information that contradicts existing beliefs (negativity bias + curiosity gap). Counter-narrative titles also attract a higher-intent audience that engages more.

### F4 — Numbered List

**Structure:** `[N] [things] [verb] [outcome]`

**Examples:**
- *5 Banking Rules That Just Got MUCH WORSE*
- *7 Things Every Retiree Must Do Before May 1*
- *13 Foods That Reverse Type 2 Diabetes (Per a specialist doctor)*

**When to use:** Almost any niche. The numbered list is the most reliable formula in YouTube history.

**When NOT to use:** Story-led documentaries where a list undermines the narrative.

**Why it works:** Numbered titles signal video structure. The viewer knows there will be N discrete chapters. Retention improves because the viewer commits to watching all N. Spotter Studio: numbered titles see 36% higher CTR and 22% higher AVD vs comparable unnumbered titles.

**Number selection rules:**
- 3-7: highest CTR (small enough to feel digestible)
- 9-13: high authority (feels comprehensive)
- 17, 23, 47: unusual numbers spike CTR via novelty
- 10, 20, 50: avoid — too manufactured-feeling

### F5 — Named Villain

**Structure:** `[Brand/person] [is doing X to Y] — [stakes]`

**Examples:**
- *Why Chase Won't Give Your Money Back After A Wire Transfer Scam*
- *Coinbase Is Quietly Locking Customer Accounts — Here's Why*
- *The IRS Just Changed Three Rules That Will Cost Retirees Thousands*

**When to use:** Finance, scam-exposure, news-hijack, true crime. Any time you have a credible institutional villain.

**When NOT to use:** When the villain framing is exaggerated. If the institution is more nuanced than "evil," the video feels like clickbait when it gets there.

**Why it works:** A named entity creates an immediate audience identification ("I bank with Chase, I need to know") and a clear emotional register (rage). Named villain titles consistently outperform abstract titles in finance and scam-exposure niches.

### F6 — Threat Alert

**Structure:** `[CONFIRMED / URGENT / WARNING]: [specific threat] [time anchor]`

**Examples:**
- *CONFIRMED: Social Security Shake-Up Hits May 1*
- *WARNING: New IRS Rule Takes Effect In 30 Days*
- *URGENT: Three Bank Closures Coming Before Year-End*

**When to use:** News-hijack, time-sensitive content, regulatory changes, deadline-driven episodes.

**When NOT to use:** Evergreen content. The threat-alert format burns out fast — use sparingly so the audience doesn't tune out the urgency signal.

**Why it works:** Combines deadline framing + authority framing + curiosity gap. The audience reads "CONFIRMED" as "the news is real" and "shake-up" as "I need to know what." A reference episode in this niche used this format and pulled significant outlier numbers.

### F7 — Forensic / Story-Led

**Structure:** `[Specific person] [verb past tense] [shocking outcome] — [hint at how]`

**Examples:**
- *He Lost $160,000 In One Phone Call — Then Chase Refused To Refund Him*
- *She Filed Social Security At 62 And Saved $246,000 — Here's How*
- *They Lost Their Restaurant After A Bank Filed One Form*

**When to use:** Single-victim narrative episodes. Strongest in finance, true crime, business, scam-exposure.

**When NOT to use:** Educational tutorials.

**Why it works:** The brain attends to specific human stories more than abstractions. A specific named or pronoun-led subject ("She," "He," "They") creates instant character investment.

### F8 — Question + Implied Answer

**Structure:** `Why does [counter-intuitive thing happen]? / What happens when [scenario]?`

**Examples:**
- *Why Do The Wealthy Never Pay Estate Tax?*
- *What Happens If You Withdraw $10,001 From Your Bank?*
- *Why Are Boomers Selling Their Homes In 2026?*

**When to use:** Educational, science, history, finance. Especially the science-explainer style of educational content.

**When NOT to use:** When you can't actually answer the question definitively. A question title that ends with "...it's complicated" kills future channel trust.

**Why it works:** Open-ended questions activate cognitive curiosity. But: question titles only outperform declaratives when the question is genuinely puzzling. Generic questions ("Why is X important?") underperform.

### F9 — Quote Attribution

**Structure:** `[Authority figure name]: "[Provocative claim]"`

**Examples:**
- *John Bogle: Why Banks Fear Customers Who Keep $20,000 In Cash*
- *Warren Buffett: The Investment Mistake That Cost Me $200 Billion*
- *Top Creator: How I Lost $30M In 2024*

**When to use:** When you have a real or credibly-paraphrased quote from an authority figure. Common in faceless / AI-narration channels.

**When NOT to use:** When the quote is fabricated. Audiences are increasingly sensitive to fabricated authority quotes; YouTube's July 2025 inauthentic content policy specifically targets fake attribution.

**Why it works:** Borrowed authority. The clicker assumes the figure said something true and important. Strong CTR mechanism but high satisfaction risk.

### F10 — Wound-First

**Structure:** `[Painful stakes statement to viewer] — [How to fix]`

**Examples:**
- *Your Retirement Will Run Out By Age 78 If You Do This*
- *You're Losing $4,000 A Year On This One IRS Mistake*
- *Your Doctor Won't Tell You About This Test*

**When to use:** Health, finance, retirement, parenting. Any niche where the audience has a specific worry the title names.

**When NOT to use:** Lifestyle, comedy, vlog. The wound-first frame doesn't fit aspirational content.

**Why it works:** Names the viewer's specific anxiety. Direct second-person ("You") outperforms third-person in CTR by approximately 10-12% (Backlinko 2024 study).

### F11 — Bracketed / Parenthetical Tag

**Structure:** `[Main title] [(Specific Modifier)]` or `[Main title] [BRACKETED LABEL]`

**Examples:**
- *5 Investing Mistakes That Cost Me $1M (Genius Strategy)*
- *I Tried Apple's New AI [HONEST REVIEW]*
- *The 2026 Social Security Schedule [UPDATED]*

**When to use:** Tech reviews, finance content, listicles. Bracketed tags signal video format and add a second curiosity gap.

**When NOT to use:** Faith, comedy, ambient. The bracketed format reads marketed.

**Why it works:** Brackets and parens consistently lift CTR by 15-38% vs identical unbracketed versions (vidIQ 2024 data). The bracket creates a second visual hook the eye lands on.

### F12 — ALL CAPS Authority

**Structure:** `[1-3 ALL CAPS WORDS] [rest of title in normal case]`

**Examples:**
- *CONFIRMED: Social Security Shake-Up Hits May 1*
- *The 10,000 Dollar Bank Rule Just Got MUCH WORSE*
- *URGENT: Three Banks To Avoid In 2026*

**When to use:** Senior finance, scam-exposure, news-hijack, threat-alert. Any niche where rage or urgency is the dominant emotional register.

**When NOT to use:** Tech, science, history, education, faith, comedy. ALL CAPS in these niches reads spammy and CTR craters.

**Why it works:** ALL CAPS signals urgency and authority simultaneously. The eye lands on caps first; if the rest of the title pays off, the click follows.

**Strict cap on ALL CAPS usage:** No more than 30% of the total title characters should be in caps. More than that triggers the spam-pattern recognition both in viewers and in YouTube's own ranking signals.

### F13 — How-To / Tutorial Direct

**Structure:** `How To [outcome] In [time/effort frame]`

**Examples:**
- *How To Read Your Social Security Statement In 5 Minutes*
- *How To Set Up A Trust In One Afternoon*
- *How To Spot An IRS Phishing Email*

**When to use:** Educational, tutorial, software, productivity, any niche where SEO match is critical.

**When NOT to use:** Story-led, narrative, opinion. How-to is the lowest CTR formula on average but the highest SEO-discoverability formula.

**Why it works:** Direct match to user search query. How-to titles win Search and Suggested traffic but underperform on Browse (the main feed).

## 4. Character count discipline

The agent sizes titles for the surface they'll appear on. There are five surfaces with different truncation cutoffs:

| Surface | Truncation cutoff | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Mobile feed (browse) | 50-60 chars | The first scroll. Most CTR happens here. |
| Mobile suggested (after-watch sidebar) | 35-45 chars | Brutal truncation. The first 5 words must carry the click. |
| Mobile search results | 50-60 chars | Same as mobile feed. |
| Desktop feed | 60-70 chars | More forgiving but desktop is <30% of total views in 2026. |
| TV / connected TV | 70+ chars (entire title shown) | Growing surface, especially for senior demos. |

**The "first 5 words" rule:** Whatever curiosity gap, dollar number, or named villain drives the click MUST be in the first 5 words. If it falls after position 5, mobile users never see it.

**Bad:** *Here's How A Couple In Iowa Lost $32,820 To The IRS — The Forfeiture Loophole*
**Good:** *$32,820 Seized — How An Iowa Grandmother Lost Her Restaurant To The IRS*

The dollar number anchors at position 1 in the second version. Truncation safe.

**Em-dash placement rule:** When using F1 (Em-Dash Mystery Gap), the dash should fall at 75-85% of total title length. Too early and the mystery payoff feels rushed. Too late and the dash gets truncated on mobile.

**Total length sweet spot:** 55-65 characters. Long enough for keywords. Short enough for mobile suggested truncation. Most outlier titles in 2025-2026 fall in this range.

### Em-dash

The em-dash (—) is the highest-CTR punctuation in YouTube titles in 2025-2026. It outperforms the colon by approximately 10-15% in matched A/B tests.

Use rules:
- **Position at 75-85% of title length.** Forces mystery payoff.
- **Use a true em-dash (—), not a hyphen (-) or two hyphens (--).** Mobile rendering matters.
- **One em-dash per title.** Two reads choppy.
- **Don't pair with a colon.** Pick one.

When NOT to use em-dash: pure tutorial / how-to titles, where the title needs to read as a search query.

### Emoji

**Default: never.** Emojis in YouTube titles in 2026 read spammy and trigger pattern recognition both in viewers and in moderate algorithm signals.

Narrow exceptions:
- 🔥 in fitness / hype gym content (single emoji, end of title only)
- 💰 in some finance hype channels (but our brand is authority, so no)
- 🚨 in extreme news-hijack (use only when CONFIRMED format isn't fitting)
- ⚠️ in compliance / legal warning content

The agent's default behavior is to never include emoji. Add only on explicit user request, and only one emoji per title.

## 7. The competitor mining protocol

Before generating a title for any video, the agent must study the niche. This is a 7-step protocol the agent runs in approximately 30 seconds:

**Step 1 — Pull top 20 outlier titles** in the same niche from the last 90 days. Define outlier as views > 3× channel median.

**Step 2 — Tag each title with formula(s) used.** Each title may use 1-2 of the 13 canonical formulas. Tag accordingly.

**Step 3 — Frequency analysis.** Which formulas appear in more than 30% of outliers? That's the dominant niche pattern. Generate primarily from that pattern.

**Step 4 — Character count cluster.** What's the median title length among outliers? The agent's generated titles should fall within ±5 characters of this median.

**Step 5 — ALL CAPS frequency.** What percentage of outlier titles use ALL CAPS? Match the distribution.

**Step 6 — Em-dash frequency.** Same analysis. Don't introduce em-dashes if outliers don't use them.

**Step 7 — Build the niche "title DNA" template.** A structured record:
```
Niche: [name]
Median character count: [X]
Top 3 formulas: [F1, F4, F12]
ALL CAPS frequency: [X%]
Em-dash frequency: [X%]
Common phrases / power words: [list]
Common villains: [list]
```

This DNA template is then loaded as system-prompt context for the title generation step.

## 8. The generation workflow

The agent generates titles using a YouTube growth strategist's discipline: **always produce 12 candidates, never fewer.** This is the working number top growth strategists use for top creators.

**Step 1 — Read the script + niche DNA.** Identify the hook architecture, the core promise, the satisfaction payoff, the specific numbers in the video.

**Step 2 — Generate 12 candidates** with deliberate spread across formulas:
- 3 from the niche's top formula
- 3 from the niche's secondary formula
- 2 from the niche's tertiary formula
- 2 wildcards (formulas not commonly used in the niche — sometimes the wildcard wins)
- 2 ALL-CAPS-and-em-dash maximalist versions

**Step 3 — Score each candidate.** Run the scoring rubric:
- Specific number present? +2
- Em-dash at 75-85% position? +2
- First 5 words carry the click? +3
- Character count 50-70? +2
- Niche formula match? +2
- ALL CAPS within 30% rule? +1
- Personal pronoun (I/you)? +1
- Anti-pattern penalties: -3 for each generic opener, click-trap, or cliché

**Step 4 — Surface top 3 + the wildcard.** Show the user the top 3 by score plus the wildcard. Always include the wildcard so the user has a non-obvious option.

**Step 5 — User picks or modifies.** User selects, edits, or asks for variation.

**Step 6 — Save the chosen title to channel memory.** The pattern that won this video becomes a higher-weighted candidate for the next video.

## 9. A/B testing discipline

YouTube's native Test & Compare runs for 4-14 days and judges variants on **watch-time-per-impression**, not raw CTR. The agent should default to setting up an A/B for every uploaded video.

**Test setup rules:**
- Use 2 variants by default. 3 if the script supports it.
- Variants should test different CTR mechanisms (e.g., one specific number, one mystery gap), not different word choices.
- Same thumbnail across variants. Title is the only variable.
- Same description, tags, scheduling, end screen.
- Don't touch for at least 7 days. Manual override only if CTR delta > 30% after 5 days.

**Watch-time-per-impression as the metric:** A title that pulls 18% CTR but loses on retention always loses to a title that pulls 11% CTR with strong retention. The agent should optimize for the latter.

## 10. Anti-patterns

Titles that fail across the board. The agent never generates these.

**A1 — Generic openers.** "How to be more productive," "5 tips for better health," "The truth about money." Vague + non-specific = ignored.

**A2 — Click-trap that doesn't pay off.** "$1 Billion Dollar Secret REVEALED" when the video shows a $5,000 lesson. Audience punishes within 30 seconds.

**A3 — Over-promising.** "This Will CHANGE YOUR LIFE." Promise too big, payoff impossible.

**A4 — Vague mystery.** "You won't believe what happened next." Curiosity gap with no specific anchor.

**A5 — Excessive ALL CAPS.** More than 30% of title characters in caps. Spam-pattern recognition.

**A6 — Emoji clutter.** "🚨 BREAKING 🔥 The IRS just BANNED 💰..." Reads as spam.

**A7 — Question-with-no-answer.** "Why is the economy collapsing? It's complicated." Dead-end.

**A8 — Year-stamping in evergreen titles.** "How to invest in 2024" dies on January 1, 2025. Use year only for genuinely time-locked content.

**A9 — Title-thumbnail mismatch.** Title says "5 reasons" and thumbnail shows a single face with no reference to 5. The pair has to be coherent.

**A10 — Channel name in title.** "[Channel Name] Reviews The 2026 Tax Code." Wastes characters; channel name is shown separately.

**A11 — Generic listicles with round numbers.** "10 best [thing]." Round numbers feel marketed.

**A12 — Subjunctive titles.** "What I Would Do If I Started Over." Subjunctive feels theoretical and underperforms.

**A13 — Self-referential titles.** "My biggest realization in 2026 was..." Audience doesn't care yet.

**A14 — Pure SEO titles.** "Best Investment Advisors For Retirees Near Me 2026" — reads robotic and underperforms on Browse, where most CTR happens.
